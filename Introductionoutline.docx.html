Investigating the Grit Construct in Engineering Education Undergraduate Programs

Introduction

  1. General background

The study of grit since the original work by Angela Duckworth (Duckworth, 2016) has been on going on in sports, education and many other applications but when looking at Grit in Engineering Education articles on this topic are scarce.  Duckworths work impacted many different areas of life she did research on national spelling bee contestants, elite athletes, military academy’s and more.  She was able though many interviews to find the key components to grit and developed a grit scale for measuring individuals grit levels (Duckworth, 2016).  This scale since that time has been used to research grit in many different arenas.  Some of the initial research on grit and engineering education has uncovered interesting findings.

  1. Specific Background

The current research of grit in Engineering Education tends to fall into different areas.  These include discussions on how to manage failure, grit tied to GPA, grit tied to program completion, grit tied to a transition to industry, and grit as a focus in senior engineering design and problem solving.  The fear of failure and the management of failure are big pieces in the puzzle when looking at student success in engineering.  Many students do not complete their degrees due to these roadblocks.  Institutions need to be aware of student’s perception of “weed out” courses that exacerbate the problem and create roadblocks for students.  The students need to learn to work through failure and grit may help with this ( Maharaj et al., 2021, Emberley  et al., 2024).  In addition to this, students often have a fear of failure that causes math anxiety, grit also has a role in math anxiety (Ogden et al., 2023).  Some strategies to address this have included a course for freshman course that attempts to teach “grit” and help students learn to work with failure. The results in this research found a small improvement in grit scores after course completion as pointed out in (Hacisalihoglu et al., 2020).  This study and others try to look at grit and its relation to student GPA (Martin et al., 2022).  Although GPA is a way to attempt to measure learning more factors may need to be considered.  Another measure of Grit and student success is degree completion and how grit can help students in this achievement (Wymer, 2018).  This might mean that grit is used more as a predictor or as part of the admissions selection process instead focusing on the possibility that grit is developed during the higher education experience. Institutions have attempted to teach grit at the end of students college careers as well, by incorporating grit into their senior project design courses as noted by  (Pierrakos, n.d.).  This article talks about how using grit as a focus in senior design along with other attributes such as motivation, self-efficacy and a sense of belonging. Instructors can act as coaches in helping students complete these courses.  In an article by (de Jager & Pott, 2022) engineers in industry specified import aspects of student learning and development during their higher education instruction.  Among these the ability to work under pressure, time management and grit were highlighted.  They talked about how important it was for students to have accurate expectations of what their future work would look like in the real world and how grit could be part of that preparation(de Jager & Pott, 2022).  

  1. Research Question

These findings are interesting but tend to show a gap in looking at how grit can be developed during the engineering education journey.  Looking at grit as a predictor of GPA is valuable but becoming a successful engineer may be a lot more complex than the story student GPAs might tell.  If students overcome failure that is tied to a poor grade that may bring down their GPA does that show grit being developed.  On the other hand, is graduating with a degree the measure?  It could be, but using grit as a predictor of who will graduate may be short sighted as well.  A program heavy with weed-out courses that only graduate those who are meant to succeed due to the grit they had when they started may be missing the boat.  There seems to be a gap in looking at what the process produces as students go through their undergraduate program. Can they grow in grit?  The study mentioned that shows an attempt at growing grit in one course at the start of the engineering undergraduate program or the other article describing a hail Mary pass of teaching grit at the end by making grit a focus in a senior design course. These studies may have their merits, but a semester is a short period of time for actual change in grit.  Research has not looked at longer periods of time, for example the entire engineering education experience of an undergrad. The purpose of this research is to ask the question: Can engineering students develop grit and grow their grit score during the entirety of their undergraduate engineering experience?

  1. Gaps in the Research

It is imaginable that grit is a characteristic we often develop through the regiment of a four-year engineering program naturally.  It would be interesting to look at the possibilities if we make attempts to help grow grit in that process as well.  If we are thorough in eliminating the concept of weed out classes and helping students work past failure as they progress their grit may be able grow more substantially during their engineering journey.  If we do it right, we have potential to make engineering education a great place to grow grit.  In her book (Duckworth, 2016)  did research on the possibly of growing grit.  The concept goes right along with Carol Dwecks work on the growth mindset (Dweek, 1986).  Duckworths research showed that grit can grow over time.  In addition, doing hard things is discussed along with explanations of how gritty people are not afraid to fail and will learn from failure as they go (Duckworth, 2016).  Completing an engineering degree definitely could qualify for doing a hard thing and each course taken would probably qualify for doing a hard thing to grow grit in the way Duckworth describes attempts for growing grit. The growth in grit that can occur in the course of completing an undergraduate engineering degree has the potential to be quite significant.  The intensity of engineering undergraduate programs is substantial.  The fortitude required for success in engineering programs is significant.  Olga  Pierrakos  explains, “Being an engineering student can be tough and usually means facing greater academic challenges. The classes are often harder, the programs are often longer, the grades are often lower, and the commitment and self-discipline required are often greater”(Pierrakos, n.d.).  Pierrakos suggest that instead of singling out “at risk” students which may make them uncomfortable and is a bit reactive we should help all students develop grit.  Grit was used in another way in the study by Maharaj and others they suggest getting a grit score of new students at orientation to find “at risk” students. Following up with group intervention and if necessary individual intervention for these students (Maharaj et al., 2021).  When studying grit we can break it down and study passion and perseverance further as individual constructs (Cho et al., 2025) .The feeling by students that professors are trying to weed out those that can’t perform can impact student response to failure (Emberley et al., 2024). Helping students feel confident and work through failure will improve student outcomes as they strive to grow their grit score.  Although recent studies have looked a different aspects of grit and its impact of what success looks like. When looking at current studies of grit and engineering students it does feel like something is missing without a view of the whole program and how grit can grow.  I think grit has an important place in engineering education and can be used for more than deciding who should be admitted because they already have grit.  It has the potential to help guide decision making as we work to provide environments where all engineering students can succeed.  Grit tends to be particularly relevant when looking at non-traditional students and academic success.  Studying grit has the potential to help us increase success for a diverse engineering education student population (Direito et al., 2021) .

V. Methodology

This will be a multi method longitudinal study that uses the grit scale and qualitative research to see the impact of grit development in engineering students in their undergraduate programs. The author attended her schooling before the research of Carol Dweck and growth mind-set research.  Because this was before the work by Carol Dweck on growth mindset (Dweck, 1986, Dweck, 2008) and Angla Duckworths research on grit (Duckworth, 2016).  These new concepts give us insight into how engineering programs have the potential to help students become gritty.
