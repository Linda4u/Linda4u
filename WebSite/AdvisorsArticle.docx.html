Article Critique: Problem Decomposition and Recomposition in Engineering Design: A Comparison of Design Behavior Between Professional Engineers, Engineering Seniors, and Engineering Freshmen

This is a critique of the article Problem Decomposition and Recomposition in Engineering Design: A Comparison of Design Behavior Between Professional Engineers, Engineering Seniors, and Engineering Freshmen by Ting Song, Kurt Becker, John Gero, Scott DeBerard, Oenardi Lawanto, & Edward Reeve (Song et al., 2016) that looks at the advanced design process of experts and seeks to show key differences by evaluating the enhanced strategies experts use against the processes used by students.   The purpose of this paper was to compare Engineering Design strategies between students and professional engineers that are expert design engineers.  Specifically, the study looked at groups or teams working together and the scope of it was to look at problem decomposition and recompostion in groups. Past studies have only looked at individuals. This study investigates the gap between skills developed in universities and skills needed in industry.

The article addressed this primary research question: In the process of engineering design, how do experts approach the design problem differently from engineering students?  The use of different design strategies plays a major role in the engineering design process and there has been a significant effort in Engineering Education to prepare students with design skills while in school.  The commonly used design process that includes problem decomposition and recompostion is a documented fundamental strategy for solving complex problems (Vincenti, 1990).

The abstract explains that convenience sampling was used to select the participants from the three groups: Engineering student Seniors, Engineering student freshmen and Engineering Experts. Participants from each category participated in a 1-hour design challenge that was video, and audio recorded. Data was collected from students and experts.  They were given a design challenge that had them address social constraints and engineering constraints at the same time.  Audio and Video recordings used to capture the conversation and interactions of the groups were later analyzed enabling evaluation of the design process. After data collection there was carful coding of the results.  Two coders were trained on the coding process.  

There were some interesting trends among the participants.  Expert Engineers were significantly better at teamwork and all parts of the design process.  Students were less adept at problem solving and tended to all be at the same level.  Senor Engineering Students did not perform better than their freshman peers in the Engineering design process.   The need to improve Student readiness in Engineering Design is paramount.  

Introduction

Interestingly the authors did not directly specify an introduction heading in this paper, but introduction information follows the keywords.  Those paragraphs read like an introduction.  They start off discussion engineering design and the significance of engineering design in engineering education.  Most studies have only been on individuals not groups when looking at engineering design.  This study not only looks at groups but makes comparisons between freshman students, senior students, and expert design engineers.  Studies show that engineering design as part of the curriculum could benefit engineering learning in many ways.  Educators tend to rush though or ignore this process not allowing adequate student growth. A common way to teach students an engineering design strategy is to break down problems into decomposition and recomposition.  The study noted a gap between novices and experts when looking at problem decomposition and recomposition.

The authors wanted to look at the gap between what engineering students are being taught and what they are learning compared to what is needed to be an expert design engineer.  Although some work has been done on this there is no information on the group design process or a good look comparing students to experts. Past studies have only focused on individuals in engineering design. This research project expanded the research into group work. It studied the group design process as opposed to studies involving individuals by recording group conversations, something that cannot be observed when studying individuals.

Section Analyses

Research Design –

Participants were chosen by convenience sampling.  Results showed preliminary findings only, due to a small N size. Engineering and social constraints were setup in the design challenge for example, only five websites related to the design challenge were allowed. The analysis od the design session was coded based on function-behavior-structure (FBS) coding scheme. Subproblems were analyzed by the level of the problem.  When the level of the problem went up it was referred to as recomposition when it went down it was defined as decomposition.

Results -

The expert engineers outperformed both engineering seniors and freshmen in problem decomposition.  They also did much more recomposition than senior and freshmen students.  Senior engineering students used problem decomposition and recomposition at the same rate as freshmen students there was no difference.

Discussion and Implications -

Even though the research settings were different i.e., groups vs. individual the results aligned quite well with the findings of (Ho, 2001) the study both show a gap in problem decomposistion and recomposition between experts and novices.  The senior and freshman student groups both spent the most time and cognitive effort on Level 3 and the least effort on Level 1.  Expert engineers on the other hand spent most of their time and cognitive effort on level 1 and on level 2 they spent more effort than the student groups.  On level 3 the expert engineers spent significantly less time than the freshman or senior engineering students.  

Contribution to Understanding

There were other interesting things that materialized from the findings. Student groups tended to generate too many or too few solutions and did not make good use of time to enhance their design as compared to expert engineers.  In addition, the expert engineers were more adept at working in teams than both student groups.  Senior Engineering groups performed better from a teamwork standpoint than the freshman engineering groups.  They Freshmen demonstrated some very significant teamwork problems.

 

Summary and Suggestions 

The gap between engineering experts and students (Atman et al., 2007) was quite pronounced.  The fact that there is no difference between freshmen and senior students in Engineering design strategies is quite concerning.  This points to a need for training during their school years in engineering design and specifically in problem decomposition and recomposition at that stage.  The expert engineers were better at seeing the big problem and analyzing the project as a whole.  Students spent less time on this stage and which was consistent with (Atman et al., 2007; Jain & Sobek, 2006  ) findings. The project brought out some interesting information on the level of expertise developed over years of working in industry.
