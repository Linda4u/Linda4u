Linda Ahlstrom Positionality

My parents gave me a wide range of life experiences. I have academic parents that worked hard in teaching me from an early age.  My mother has a master’s degree from Berkley and my father has a master’s degree from UCSF.  My family lived in married student housing in San Fransico when I was young. We moved from California to the four corners area. My Dad was an instructor for adult Navajo students.  I had a close group of Navajo friends that I spent recess with at school.  My Mom occasionally flew on small planes to extremely remote locations on the reservation teaching much needed nutrition classes. From there we moved to the north shore of Hawaii.  I had friends there from everywhere it seemed. All the pacific islands New Zealand, Australia, Samoa, Tahiti, Tonga, Figi, and many places in Asia. One summer while living in Hawaii we took a trip to Guatemala where we lived on a plantation, and I went to school with kids and a teacher that did not speak any English.  

My childhood years were before the research of Carol Dweck and her growth mind-set findings.  I think my mom had a growth mind-set. She went to bat for me a few times in elementary school to make sure my teachers did not limit my growth. I can understand well Carol Dwecks description of the way things were in her growing up years. I did not quite see the extremes she did like students being seated by IQ but I saw enough in my day to understand what she experienced. By the time I attended school they were just transitioning away from the college track vs. trade track routes.  Students were no longer being treated in the extremes of Carol Dweck’s day but, here were still remnants from the past.

In science class one day my teacher was watching me carefully doing a lab that involved beakers and he said “Linda I can see you are going to become a nurse someday”.  I remember thinking “A NURSE???” In my mind, I was shouting “A NURSE?”.  I remember thinking to myself “of all the comments he could have made, why did he say that?”  I am sure I was thinking “I am going to get lots more education and do a lot more than a nurse would do”. I think nursing is a great profession. The point is, that at that time girls where thought of as being on secretary or nurse track in school.

Getting an education in Engineering was a place where I put to use my strong belief I could do anything I set my mind to, to work.  This became the critical time in life that impacted how I know what I know (Secules et al., 2021). I overcame challenges like not having taken any chemistry in high school. Because this was before the work by Carol Dweck on growth mindset (Dweek, 1986), (Dweck, 2008). I wrestled with the concept of “smart” wondering if other students were smarter than I was and unsure if I could grow academically at times.

Looking at the Thycol writeup brought back vivid undergraduate memories. There are instances in time when you can remember exactly what you were doing at that moment.  For many years it was something you asked others “where were you when the space shuttle blew up?”  I was at Weber State finishing my senior year of college.  I was at Weber for one year. I transferred there after completing most of my engineering undergraduate work at Utah State. I was in the engineering building when I heard that the space shuttle blew up.  I distinctly remember walking down the hall with silence all around me the mood was so heavy the hall was eerily quiet.  Everyone was in shock at what had just happened.  Our teachers seemed overcome with grief and the heaviness of the circumstances.  I have that picture and moment embedded in my mind.  Reading the space shuttle article brought everything back and shed light on the situation in a new way.  

At Weber State at the time my professors worked at different companies. They did teaching, then working in industry, rotations.  A large majority of those rotations were at Thycol.  There was a professor and a group of students working on a satellite in conjunction with Thycol that eventually went into space.  After reading the article I wondered about the long faces I remember seeing in the engineering building that day.  Did they automatically think about the implications for Thycol? Did they know the engineers who worked on the boosters? Were they aware that the engineers had asked NASA not to launch?  I have to think there were more connections to the event than I originally thought as a senior student trying to rush through her senior year and graduate. There are other flashbacks the article brought to mind. I know the climate and types on pressure management were putting on engineers during that time period.  

 I was recruited by Northrop at Weber state and went off to work in California less 6 months after the space shuttle blew up.  There were many managers at Northrop without formal education that moved up through the school of hard knocks. Many of them were former military.  They would through their weight around quite often and did not always want to follow engineering protocol or listen to the facts brought to the forefront by engineering teams.  I had a particular experience that highlighted this climate.  I was working in the wire harness shop on rotation and was doing a lot of liaison work between the wire harness fabrication and the engineering team that designed the wire harnesses for he the large military aircraft we were building.  I would check CAD models of the wires in the plane on my computer (we sat at monitors in large rooms that were connected to a big mainframe with the CAD design of the plane). There were no personal computers yet. We would print out large sheets that became the “tool” to build the wire harness. One day I noticed there were two cables of wire that did not connect to another part or wire harness in the CAD system.  I went to talk with the engineers and ask where the wires were supposed to connect.   The process to keep the wire harness production line going would involve me hand drawing a change on the large life size print out of the wire harness on the shop floor and then putting my stamp ( an actual stamp I possessed as an engineer). This drawing with my hand drawn correction to the CAD printout would be used to build the wire harness right away.  I went and talked to an engineer on another engineering team he said, “we don’t know where those wires go.”  My next question was can we make the change to the drawing without your fix in the CAD system and he said, “if you will let us.”  I knew he meant we should not let them just say we don’t know where these go and go and build it anyway.  I went back to the bosses I was temporarily working for and said, “we need to stop production until they figure out where these wires go.”  My boss got red in the face and said, “you draw in the correction and stamp it we are not holding up production for this.”  He was under a lot of pressure from people above him to keep things moving.  I said “no” and then he turned to two of his employees that were junior to me and said, “make the correction and stamp it.”  They both said “no” if Linda doesn’t think we should do it we won’t do it.  The boss who had left his own stamp at home that day was furious and threatened to fire me.  I still said “no” then his boss a VP yelled at me I sill said “no”.  It turned out things were quite political in the manufacturing program and the boss over my training program called me in and said, “Linda I am so proud of you”.  Her husband was a VP in a neighboring department and they both thought the VP that yelled at me was wrong and out of line.  I can see how the Thycol engineers recommendations to postpone the launch due to temperature constraints was ignored by high level bosses that would not listen to reason.  Later in my time at Northrop the VP that I said, “no” to said to me when I gave him the news I was leaving Northrop to finish my Electrical Engineering masters at Cal State Long Beach , “ You don’t need your masters in formal education! You need your masters of experience.” He had a fixed mind set when it came to learning and growth.

During my engineering career I had an incredible opportunity at a young age to be on the IEEE continuing education committee.  This was a great honor but became an interesting/frustrating experience when I tried to tell them “This brand-new thing, the internet, is going to change learning and teaching it will change everything” the rest of the committee was comprised of older men.  They would say, “we have seen it before, the latest “new” thing. Nothing is going to change. Teaching and learning will stay the same as it has always been."  I think this experience still motivates me and my research choice. I probably tend to approach things a bit as “not again.”  I want to make sure people are not caught off guard and are ready for the next epic shift in the way we will learn.  

I recently watch a movie called the Rule Breakers about girls from Afghanistan that built robots for competitions and traveled all over competing.  It rang true to me, not because I didn’t have money for a computer in my twenties or that girls were not allowed to use them.  Personal computers did not exist.  There were main frames that we could program and that is how I learned to code as an undergrade at Utah State.  My senior year of college we started caring around suitcases with a microprocessors in them. They had enough parts put together for a cryptic system where we could input code and carry around a crude version of a portable personal computer.  We were before our time. Over the next few years I watched as computers became accessible to everyone in the US.  To think girls in Afghanistan are still not allowed to touch computers is so shocking.  This makes me more determined than ever to forward the growth mind set though out the world and make access to learning accessible.  If reliable AI tools could be developed that help excellerate learning and growth everywhere by giving access to learning in romote locations it would be fantastic.  Several years ago a friend of mine and I gathered donation of shoes for school children in Costa Rica.  We used her JetBlue passes  to take lots of suitcases of shoes there.  The children in Costa Rica can not participate in school if they don’t have the right shoes.  We drove around romote out skirts of the town where I saw a 10-year-old boy in a jungle setting laying on a mat in an old rickety shack with several small rooms on his smart phone.  That picture stays in my mind we need to get access to learning to everyone.
